Here’s Why The Times’s First Crossword Is Nearly Impossible to Solve Today

This story is part of a series marking 175 years of eyewitness journalism from The New York Times. Read more here.

The New York Times ran its first-ever crossword puzzle 84 years ago. Titled “Headlines and Footnotes,” the grid — 23 rows stacked neatly with a smattering of black boxes — boasted the bylines of Charles Erlenkotter, a seasoned constructor, and Margaret Farrar, the puzzle editor.

The paper had previously turned up its nose at such pursuits. But, in the wake of the 1941 attack on Pearl Harbor, Lester Markel, The Times’s Sunday editor, decided it was time to give a little. In a memo to Arthur Hays Sulzberger, the paper’s publisher, Markel urged a bold pivot. “We ought to proceed with the puzzle,” he wrote, framing it as an invitation to readers, fretting over a world at war, to partake in a bit of playfulness.

But we’d venture that even the most hard-core solvers in 2026 would struggle to fill the entire grid. Even Will Shortz, The Times’s lead Crossword editor since 1993, has no trouble admitting that it’s a doozy. It simply has “too many things I don’t think I should have to know,” he said.

Erlenkotter set the tone immediately with 1-Across [Famous one-eyed general], which references Archibald WAVELL of the British Army, who served in the military for over four decades. Later, at 30-Across [Name of 3 successive Pharaohs] solves for a moniker that’s hardly on the tip of anyone’s tongue: USERTESEN. This answer has not appeared in a single New York Times Crossword since.

Though Shortz does his best to imbue today’s grids with a timeless quality, every puzzle is ultimately an artifact of its era — not quite frozen in amber but certainly reflective of the culture in which it was curated. Even Shortz’s puzzles from the 1990s include trivia that solvers now might have long forgotten. A look at the very first edition makes clear just how much the Crossword has evolved over the past eight decades.

With the 1942 puzzle, Farrar established several principles that still remain intact. Rotational black square symmetry — the puzzle can be turned 180 degrees to yield the same pattern — holds true today. In a broad sense, the structure of the cluing is also similar: All clues must follow the same parts of speech as their answers. [Passageways] are STREETS (48-Across) and [Fry quickly] is SAUTE (47-Across). The clues also avoid using “to” at the front so things substitute perfectly: (to) “fry quickly” = (to) “sauté.”

Modern standards dictate lively vocabulary, a focus on general knowledge and pop culture and the requirement that all answers, especially the less familiar ones, don’t intersect with other hard-to-guess words.

In 1942, the world of wordsmithing was decidedly different. The Times’s crossword puzzle was meant to be more “newsy” than those published by competitors, intended to mesh well with its canvas. Despite having no obvious theme, the first puzzle was loosely spun together around of-the-era news content. In it, you’ll find references to cannons, naval bases, defense stamps and not one but three matter-of-fact mentions of the Nazis, such as 103-Down: [Port on Zuider Zee, occupied by Nazis], the clue for AMSTERDAM.

According to Shortz, Farrar wasn’t actually fond of the instructions she received to make the puzzle part and parcel of the newspaper’s journalism.

“When no one was looking, she started to downplay the amount of news and increase the number of literary references and other things,” he said. It’s an ethos he still follows, sprinkling in clues and answers that feature movie titles, candy brands and internet slang, for example. “Nowadays, everything in life is fair game for a crossword,” he said. While Farrar liked to avoid any references to death, disease and taxes, Shortz believes one or two references to serious matters are permissible in the puzzle. Ultimately, though, “you do want the crossword to be upbeat and to leave people with a good feeling,” he said.

Among the many differences in the debut grid, Shortz points out that it is chock-full of obscure names, prefixes and suffixes and odd forms of words, like 87-Across’s REDENOTES — as in, [Designates again]. (An online search returns fewer than 1,000 hits for the term.) To put an entry in a modern grid such as GENF at 98-Across — the German and Swiss-German spelling of Geneva — “would be desperation only,” Shortz said.

A Shakespeare reference that does not say it’s a Shakespeare reference would also most likely prove to be a trap for today’s solvers. That’s 81-Across: [“I am ___ Oracle”], the answer for which is a remark from “The Merchant of Venice” with not even a hint at its provenance. (The answer is SIR.)

“Maybe they just expected solvers in those days to know their Shakespeare and to know that quote,” Shortz said. “But if we were to run that today, that clue wouldn’t be off limits, but I would explain where it came from so solvers feel comfortable after they get the answer.”

Early grids did not have themes or playful clues, instead focusing on history, geography, literature and other classical fields of knowledge. This might be why FRAIL at 97-Across is not an adjective meaning “weak and delicate” but rather [Basket made of rushes], and YVON, at 66-Across, is simply [Man’s name].

Shortz dryly gives the grid due credit for being made in the “precomputer age” and for its “nice vocabulary.” Puzzles then were made without assistance from an online crossword-filling program or search engine to discover new terms. It’s only natural to discover tough words pulled straight from the dictionary, like REDOTTED [Sketched lightly again], LASSITUDE [Weariness] and BISTRE [Dark brown color].

Other shifts are more subtle. Take UMBRELLA at 77-Down. Today, crossword editors love to focus on a lively angle, such as the 2013 clue [Sidewalk cafe sight] or the trickier [Kind of insurance policy], first used in 1999. A 2007 acrostic puzzle even used [Prop for a filmed dance by Gene Kelly]. But in the first Times crossword, the clue is [Symbol of appeasement], possibly a nod to the British prime minister Neville Chamberlain’s famous rolled umbrella, carried during negotiations for the 1938 Munich Agreement. Talk about a deep cut.

There’s no rule against including a tough clue in a crossword. In fact, some editors might argue that a successful grid teaches you something new. It’s a point of pride for Shortz that the modern puzzle flexes knowledge that he himself might not have known the week before. This is in part thanks to a wide and varied pipeline of constructors and scrappy editors who nitpick each grid.

Shortz believes that the crossword puzzle has worked its way into solvers’ hearts via the emotional “aha” moment of figuring out an answer. For an activity that seems methodical and academic, solving can also be private and, therefore, sentimental.

Crosswords “take you into nearly every field of human knowledge,” Shortz says, which whets the appetite for more learning and gives the most studied solvers a foothold at, say, the opera, or the Emmy Awards, or the U.S. Open.

By November 1942, nearly nine months after Erlenkotter’s debut grid in The Times, the weekly feature had inspired passionate debate among the many solvers who wrote letters about whether to publish the solutions in the same issue of the paper. “It’s good for morale to know the answers,” one solver from Pennsylvania wrote. “So many puzzles in the world are never answered.”
